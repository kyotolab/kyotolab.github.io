Spot guide 14

Philosopher’s Path

TETSUGAKU-NO-MICHI

The name comes from a philosopher, the cherry trees from a painter. It is simply a path beside a canal.

When to go

It is free and always open. But the path is narrow, and at midday in cherry blossom season (usually early April) and in the autumn leaves you just shuffle along behind the person in front. In those weeks, start walking by about 8:00.

There are about 400 cherry trees. The few days after the blossoms fall, when the petals cover the water, are just as good. In summer there is plenty of shade, so it works even at noon.

What to look at

The canal itself. When the canal bringing water from Lake Biwa was finished in 1890, this path was built for the people who looked after it. The water beside you has just crossed the top of the brick aqueduct at Nanzen-ji.

The cherry trees are called “Kansetsu cherries.” In 1921 the painter Hashimoto Kansetsu, who lived nearby, and his wife Yone gave 300 cherry saplings to the city. Almost all of the first trees have been replaced since, but the name stayed.

Good to know

The name comes from Kyoto University philosophers such as Nishida Kitaro and Tanabe Hajime, who walked here while they were thinking. Local residents settled on the name “Philosopher’s Path” in 1972. Near Honen-in, halfway along, a stone carries one of Nishida’s poems.

The path runs through a residential area. Please keep your voice down and do not step onto private property for photos.

How to fit it in

The north end is near Ginkaku-ji; the south end is around Nyakuoji Bridge, a short walk from Nanzen-ji. See Ginkaku-ji first thing in the morning, then walk south to Nanzen-ji. It makes a half day with almost no buses.

At a brisk pace it takes about 30 minutes. If you stop at Honen-in or the small temples off the path, allow an hour.
