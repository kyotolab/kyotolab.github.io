Kyoto Lab ／ Field notes

Translation won’t fix it

Travellers get stuck in Kyoto’s small shops all the time. I took apart two scenes I saw with my own eyes to figure out why. Short version: in most places, what’s missing isn’t translation. You can only translate what has been written down.

What I saw

Two ways to get stuck.

Not guesses. Both happened in front of me, here in Kyoto. And they are two different kinds of stuck.

CASE A ／ Public bathhouse

The information is there. Nobody moves.

The changing room of a neighbourhood bathhouse had its rules posted in English. Even so, a visitor from abroad was frozen in place. They could read the signs just fine. Reading them simply didn’t tell them what to do next.

How much do you take off? Where do you wash? What happens to the towel? Every rule was written down, one by one. The order was written nowhere.

→ There are words, but no steps

CASE B ／ Barber

There’s no information at all.

A traveller walked in and asked if they could get a haircut right now. The shop only takes bookings, so the owner said no. The owner doesn’t speak English, so it all happened in gestures.

The traveller wasted a trip. The shop lost time it might have sold. From outside the door, there was no way to tell whether they had a free chair.

→ Before any steps, you can’t even see the state of the shop

The point

English isn’t what’s missing.

A translation app fixes neither case. In Case A, the text was already in English. In Case B, there was no text to translate in the first place.

What they share is this: the things a shop assumes “any Japanese person just knows” are written down nowhere. The order of things, the customs nobody was ever taught but everyone picked up. None of it is on paper, so there is nothing to translate.

Translation can’t translate what was never written.
Add a translation tool, and the problem stays.

Breaking it down

Three things a traveller wants to know at the door.

Take the problems on the ground apart and they boil down to three questions. None of them gets answered by translating text.

Q1

Can I come in?

Do I need a booking, or can I just walk in? Are they open today? Is this a place I’m allowed into? (Is it regulars only? Are kids OK? What about tattoos?)

You can’t tell from outside, so it’s either give up or risk being a nuisance

Q2

What happens inside?

The order of things from the door to the exit. Where you pay, what you take off, where you sit, what you mustn’t do. How long it takes and roughly what it costs.

A sign full of rules only tells you what not to do. What you should do is still a mystery

Q3

Should I go now?

Is it busy right now? How long is the wait? If it’s packed, is there something similar nearby?

Nobody knows, so everyone ends up at the same famous spots at the same hour

Fixes

Three questions, three tools.

One tool per question. But Kyoto Lab is run by one person, so the test isn’t whether I can build it. It’s whether it keeps working without putting any load on the shop.

Answers Q2 ／ Build first

Steps: hand over what happens inside, in order

Stop listing rules. Rebuild the visit, from the door to the exit, as a numbered list of actions. The trick is to hand over the order, not the text. Stick up one QR code and the steps show up on the customer’s phone, in their language.

Good for
Bathhouses, hot spring baths, restaurants with a way of doing things, sweet shops, temple visits, workshops
Shop effort
Once. Set it up and it keeps running with the owner doing nothing
Customer effort
Scan the QR, pick a language, read from the top. That’s it
Not for
Places where the steps change for every customer (mostly complex bookings or one-to-one service)
Why this comes first: it’s the only one of the three that keeps working without the owner touching it every day. When you run things alone, any tool that needs the shop’s daily effort will stop sooner or later. And it solves, head-on, the bathhouse scene that got Kyoto Lab started.
Answers Q1 ／ Second

Now: put “can I come in?” outside the door

Not a booking system. The owner presses one of three buttons a few times a day: “Come on in,” “About a 20-minute wait,” “Closed for today.” That shows up on the QR code by the door and on the shop’s map listing, in several languages.

Good for
Barbers, family-run restaurants, local massage clinics, small craft workshops
Shop effort
Every day. This is the weak spot. Forget to press it and the information becomes a lie. Skip enough days and the whole tool dies
Customer effort
Scan the QR at the door, or check before heading out
Design rule
If updates stop, fall back to “no information” automatically. Showing stale information does more harm than showing nothing
Why it waits: the payoff is big, but it leans on the owner’s daily effort. Bring it in after the first shop already has Steps and there’s a relationship with the owner, and it has a much better chance of lasting.
Answers Q3 ／ Not an app

Shift: spread the crowds across time and place

When people say Kyoto is crowded, what’s actually crowded is the same handful of places at the same hours. For one person, handing out knowledge of the quiet hours and the alternatives is more realistic than measuring crowds.

For
Everyone who travels to Kyoto
Why not an app
One person can’t realistically collect live crowd data, and an inaccurate crowd map is the kind of failure that loses your trust in one go
Instead
Solve it with writing. “Kiyomizu-dera opens at 6 a.m.” “Okazaki’s streets are wide, so it never looks crowded.” Knowledge like that is faster, more accurate and slower to go stale as an article
The link
Every article spreads the crowds a little more. The more I write, the better it works
This one isn’t a tool, it’s publishing. It’s the only one of the three where not building anything was the right answer. The spot guides and field notes on this site are that fix in action. (Opening times change, so check official information before you go.)

Comparison

Ranked by: can one person keep it running?

The yardstick isn’t how big the effect is, but whether it keeps running without stopping. If you’re holding it up alone, that column matters most.

How to read this table

The interesting one is Now. It’s the easiest to build, and it still comes second. Easy to build and easy to keep going are two different things. A tool the owner has to press every day doesn’t get pressed on a busy day. Information that isn’t updated becomes a lie. And a tool that lied to you once never gets used again.

In a one-person business, staying power ends up being the only yardstick that counts. A tool that stops working isn’t worth zero. It’s worth less than zero.
