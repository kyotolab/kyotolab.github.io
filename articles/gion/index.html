What You're Actually Looking At

In Gion,
the Photo You Want
May Be Illegal

There is a ¥10,000 fine for photography on some streets in Gion and none at all on others. Nothing on the ground tells you which street you are standing on.

Gion · Higashiyama-kuKyotoKyoto Lab field note

What visitors assumeA preserved historic quarter, open to walk through and photograph.
What is actually thereA working district of private streets, private houses, and people going to work — with one public road running through the middle.

Since April 2024, the residents' and businesses' association in Gion has prohibited photography in the private alleys around Hanamikoji street. The stated penalty is ¥10,000.

Hanamikoji itself — the wide, famous lane with the wooden facades — is a public road, and cannot be closed to photography in the same way.

So the rule is: on this street, photographs are fine. Turn down that gap between two buildings, and they are not.

Now stand there and try to tell which is which.

The real problem

A rule you cannot see is not a rule you can follow

The alleys of Gion do not look like private property to a visitor. They have no gates. They are paved like the main street. They are, frankly, more beautiful than the main street, which is exactly why people walk into them.

In Japan the boundary is understood without a sign. The width of the lane, the way the stone changes, the lanterns belonging to specific houses — locals read all of it instantly and never think about it.

A visitor reads none of it. They are not being disrespectful. They are being unable to see something that was never made visible.

This is the pattern behind almost every friction between Kyoto and its visitors, and it is worth stating plainly, because it changes what you do about it:

The rules are not hidden by intent. They were simply never written down, because for four hundred years everyone already knew them.

Translating a sign does not fix this. There is no sign to translate.

What a hanamachi is

Not performers. Commuters.

Kyoto has five hanamachi — districts where geiko and their apprentices, maiko, live, train, and work. Gion Kobu is the largest. The others are Gion Higashi, Miyagawa-cho, Pontocho and Kamishichiken.

Kyoto uses the word geiko, not "geisha." They are artists under long professional training — dance, shamisen, song, conversation — and they work in the teahouses of the district in the evening.

The woman in the kimono crossing the street at 5:40pm is not part of an attraction. She is late for work, in shoes that make running impossible, and a group of strangers has just formed a wall in front of her with phones up.

That is the behaviour the rule exists to stop. It escalated to people touching sleeves and blocking doorways, which is why a fine now exists at all.

Most people in kimono in Gion during the day, incidentally, are visitors in rental kimono. The photograph you think you are taking is usually of another tourist.

Instead

Three ways to actually meet this district

Go to the theatre. Every April, Gion Kobu stages the Miyako Odori, a dance performance run several times a day for the month, at the district's own theatre. It is a public performance, tickets are sold, photography rules are stated, and you see the art rather than chasing it down a lane. Other hanamachi hold their own seasonal dances.

Walk Shirakawa instead of Hanamikoji. A few minutes north, the Shirakawa canal runs past willow trees and old wooden houses. Same architecture, small stone bridges, a fraction of the crowd, and no ambiguity about where you may stand.

Come at the wrong hour. Gion at 7am is a normal neighbourhood: deliveries, cleaning, cats. The light is better and nobody is being photographed at work.

The geography, briefly

Where Gion actually is

Gion runs along Shijo street, with Yasaka Shrine closing the eastern end and the Kamo River at the western end. Yasaka Shrine is open, free, and busy at night — it is the one part of the area designed for crowds.

Between them, the blocks north and south of Shijo are ordinary streets where people live. That is the whole distinction, and once you hold it, the district stops being confusing.

Notes
The fine
Set by the local association for private lanes near Hanamikoji, in force since April 2024. Hanamikoji itself is a public road and is treated differently. Signage exists but is easy to walk past.
Asking for a photo
If a geiko or maiko is walking, she is working. If you are a guest at a teahouse or a booked event, photography is arranged for you. There is no third case.
Yasaka Shrine
At the eastern end of Shijo, open at night, no admission. The reasonable place to spend evening time in this area.
Check before you go
Miyako Odori dates and ticketing are published each year by Gion Kobu. Confirm rather than assuming April availability.
