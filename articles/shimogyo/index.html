Living in Shimogyo, Kyoto

The ward I was born in — the grid, the food, the river, and one extremely hot festival.

I was born in Shimogyo-ku, Kyoto. I left for six years for work and came back at thirty, and in those six years the neighbourhood changed more than I expected. So this is the part of Kyoto I know best, and the part I keep finding myself explaining to people who visit.

Most people come to Kyoto for the temples. Shimogyo doesn't really have the famous ones. What it has is the part of Kyoto where people actually live and work — and, as it happens, the part most visitors end up sleeping in without quite realising where they are.

The city part of the old capital

Kyoto Station is in Shimogyo. So is a good stretch of Shijo, the main shopping street. If you have arrived by shinkansen, walked out of the station and thought this is more of a city than I was expecting — that is Shimogyo.

It catches people off guard. Kyoto has an image of wooden houses and moss gardens, and that Kyoto does exist, but it is mostly east and north of here. Shimogyo is department stores, offices, underground shopping arcades and a great many bicycles.

You will not get lost here

Kyoto is built on a grid. Streets run north to south and east to west, and almost all of them have names. Once you understand that an address like "Shijo-Karasuma" simply means the crossing of Shijo street and Karasuma street, the whole city opens up and you can more or less put your phone away.

Children here learn the east-west streets through a song called Maru-Take-Ebisu, which lists them in order from north to south. I wrote about it in an earlier post. I still use it. Plenty of adults still count streets under their breath.

Cheap food, one street off the main road

Because this is a working part of town — offices, shops, students — there are a lot of places to eat, and they are not priced for tourists. Lunch sets, standing sushi, coffee shops that have not changed their interiors since about 1970, ramen near the station at midnight.

My one rule for visitors: walk one street off the main road. Shijo-dori and the station building are perfectly fine, but both the prices and the queues drop the moment you turn a corner.

A river you can run along

The Kamo River runs down the eastern edge of the ward. There are paths on both banks, and you can run for kilometres without crossing a road. Early morning is the best of it: herons, a few people stretching, almost nobody else.

This is the thing I miss when I travel. A long, open, free piece of ground in the middle of a city, where nobody is trying to sell you anything.

If you are booking a hotel, start here

Practical advice, and the main reason I wanted to write this.

If you are looking for somewhere to stay in Kyoto, search Shimogyo first — around Kyoto Station and Shijo-Karasuma in particular. There are more hotels here than in any other part of the city, at every price level, and you are sitting on top of the subway, bus and rail network that takes you everywhere else.

Staying somewhere famously beautiful sounds lovely right up until you are waiting forty minutes for a bus in the rain.

And then there is July

Gion Matsuri takes over the city every July, and hundreds of thousands of people come for it.

Something visitors often don't know: although the festival carries the name of Gion, the great yamahoko floats belong to neighbourhoods right here, around Shijo-Karasuma. In the days before the procession they are assembled out on the street, by hand, using only rope — no nails at all — in a technique called nawagarami. You can walk straight up and stand next to one while it is being built. For my money that is better than the procession itself.

Two warnings. The first is the heat. July in Kyoto is humid in a way that has to be experienced rather than described, and the crowds do not help.

The second is that people here will tell you it always rains during Gion Matsuri. This turns out to be less superstition than arithmetic: the main procession falls on 17 July, and the rainy season in this region historically ends around 21 July. The festival is scheduled, on average, four days too early. Bring a light rain jacket rather than an umbrella — in that crowd, nobody thanks you for an umbrella.

Come and see it

Shimogyo is not the Kyoto on the postcards. It is the Kyoto where the postcards get printed, delivered and sold, and where the people who make them go home at night.

It is a very good place to live and an extremely practical place to stay. If you make it here, I would genuinely like to hear how it went — please leave a comment.
