Living in Shimogyo, Kyoto

The ward I was born in — the grid, the food, the river, and one extremely hot festival.

Have you ever stepped off the shinkansen at Kyoto Station, walked outside and thought, huh, this is more of a city than I expected?

That's Shimogyo, the ward I was born in.

I left Kyoto for six years for work and came back at thirty. In those six years the city changed more than I expected. Even so, this is the Kyoto I know best, and the Kyoto I can't stop explaining to anyone who visits.

Most people come to Kyoto for the temples. Shimogyo does have Higashi Hongan-ji and Nishi Hongan-ji, but very little of the "postcard Kyoto" of Kiyomizu-dera or Kinkaku-ji. What it has instead is the part of the city where Kyoto people actually live and work. It is also where a lot of visitors end up sleeping without quite realising it.

If you're looking for somewhere to stay in Kyoto, here's the conclusion up front: start your search in Shimogyo. I'll explain why further down.

The city part of the old capital

Kyoto Station is in Shimogyo. So is a good stretch of Shijo-dori, the main shopping street.

Kyoto means streets of wooden machiya townhouses and moss-covered gardens. That Kyoto is real, but it's mostly east and north of here. Shimogyo is department stores, offices, underground shopping arcades and an astonishing number of bicycles. First-time visitors are usually caught off guard by the gap.

You can't get lost here

Kyoto is laid out on a grid. The streets run dead straight, north to south and east to west, and almost all of them have names.

"Shijo-Karasuma" isn't the name of a district. It's the crossing of Shijo-dori and Karasuma-dori. Once that clicks, the whole city becomes readable and you'll barely need the map on your phone. The words agaru and sagaru that appear in addresses simply mean "go north" and "go south".

Kyoto children learn the east-west streets with a nursery rhyme called Maru-Take-Ebisu. "Maru, take, ebisu, ni, oshi, oike..." It runs through the street names in order, from north to south.

I still use it. Plenty of grown-ups in Kyoto are quietly counting streets under their breath.

Cheap food, one street off the main road

Shimogyo is a working part of town: offices, shops, students. That means there are a huge number of places to eat, and they aren't priced for tourists.

Daily lunch sets, standing sushi bars, coffee shops whose interiors haven't changed since about 1970, ramen in front of Kyoto Station at midnight.

I give visitors only one rule: walk one street off the main road. Shijo-dori and the Kyoto Station building are perfectly fine. But the moment you turn a corner, the prices and the queues both drop.

A river you can run along for kilometres, no traffic lights

The Kamo River runs down the eastern edge of Shimogyo. There are paths on both banks, and you can run for kilometres without crossing a single road.

Early morning is the best of it. Herons, a few people stretching, and almost nobody else.

This is what I miss when I travel: a long, open, free space right in the middle of the city. A place where nobody is trying to sell you anything.

Book your Kyoto hotel in Shimogyo first

Now for the practical part, which is honestly the main reason I wrote this.

If you're looking for somewhere to stay in Kyoto, search Shimogyo first, especially around Kyoto Station and Shijo-Karasuma.

Shimogyo has more hotels than any other ward in Kyoto. The price range is wide, so there are options for every budget. And you'll be staying right on top of the transport network that takes you everywhere else in the city.

  • Around Kyoto Station: the shinkansen, JR, Kintetsu, the subway and city buses all meet here. Unbeatable on the day you arrive and the day you leave.
  • Around Shijo-Karasuma: where the Hankyu line meets the subway. You'll never be short of places to eat or shop.

And then comes July

Every July, Kyoto is taken over by Gion Matsuri. The festival runs for the whole month and draws hundreds of thousands of people.

Here's something surprisingly few people know. Although it's called the "Gion" festival, the great yamahoko floats don't belong to the Gion district. They belong to neighbourhoods around Shijo-Karasuma, in Shimogyo and Nakagyo wards.

A few days before the procession, the floats are assembled right out on the street. It's all done by hand, without a single nail: they're bound tight with rope alone, a technique called nawagarami. You can walk straight up to a float while it's being built and watch from close by. Personally, I'd recommend this over the procession itself.

Two warnings.

The first is the heat. Kyoto sits in a basin, and July humidity here is on another level. It has to be experienced rather than described. Then add the crowds.

The second is the rain. People in Kyoto will tell you it always rains during Gion Matsuri. That's less superstition than arithmetic.

The Saki Matsuri procession is on 17 July. The rainy season in the Kinki region ends, on average, around 19 July. So in an average year, the festival comes just before the rainy season is over. The Ato Matsuri procession on 24 July, by contrast, usually falls after it.

For the rain, bring a light rain jacket rather than an umbrella. In that crowd, nobody will thank you for opening one.

The Kyoto that isn't on the postcards

Shimogyo isn't the Kyoto on the postcards. It's the Kyoto where the postcards are printed, delivered and sold, and where the people who make them go home at night.

It's a very good place to live, and an extremely convenient place to stay.
